Fixed-point method for solving airflow networks#

1. Physical principles: balance equations and constitutive laws#

An airflow network represents the movement of air through openings, cracks, ducts, or other flow paths connecting different pressure nodes. The network is governed by two fundamental types of equations:

  1. Balance equations, expressing conservation of mass at the pressure nodes.

  2. Constitutive laws, describing the relationship between the airflow rate through a branch and the pressure difference across it.

These equations form a nonlinear system because the airflow through an opening generally depends nonlinearly on the pressure difference.

1.1. Mass conservation at pressure nodes#

Consider a network composed of (N) internal pressure nodes connected by (B) branches. Each branch connects two nodes or an internal node to a reference-pressure node.

At each internal node, conservation of mass requires that the algebraic sum of the mass flow rates through the connected branches, together with any imposed mass-flow source, be zero.

Using a consistent orientation for the branches, the mass balance equations can be written as

[ A^T\dot{\boldsymbol m}{=tex}+\boldsymbol {=tex}f=0 ]

where:

  • (A) is the branch-node incidence matrix;

  • (\dot{\boldsymbol m}{=tex}) is the vector of branch mass flow rates (kg/s);

  • (\boldsymbol {=tex}f) is the vector of imposed mass-flow sources at internal nodes (kg/s).

The sign convention is that positive branch mass flow follows the orientation assigned to the branch. A positive component of (\boldsymbol {=tex}f) represents a mass-flow sink under this convention, whereas a negative component represents a mass-flow source.

The balance equations express the fact that air cannot accumulate at a pressure node in a steady-state airflow network.

1.2. Nonlinear constitutive law: volumetric flow and pressure difference#

The airflow through an opening or leakage path is commonly described by a power-law relationship between the volumetric flow rate and the pressure difference across the opening:

[ \dot {=tex}V_{ij}=K_{ij}, \operatorname{sign}{=tex}(\Delta {=tex}p_{ij}) |\Delta {=tex}p_{ij}|^{n_{ij}} ]

where:

  • (\dot {=tex}V_{ij}) is the volumetric flow rate (m³/h);

  • (K_{ij}) is the flow coefficient (m³/(h·Pa(^{n_{ij}})));

  • (\Delta {=tex}p_{ij}) is the pressure difference across the branch (Pa);

  • (n_{ij}) is the flow exponent, with (0<n_{=tex});

  • (\operatorname{sign}{=tex}(\Delta {=tex}p_{ij})) indicates the direction of the flow.

The flow exponent depends on the characteristics of the opening and the flow regime. Typical values include:

  • (n=0.5): turbulent flow, commonly used for openings;

  • (n) approaching 1: predominantly laminar flow.

For (n=0.5), the flow is proportional to the square root of the absolute pressure difference. Consequently, doubling the pressure difference does not double the volumetric flow rate.

The sign function is essential because the pressure difference can change sign, causing the flow direction to reverse.

The constitutive law can also be expressed as a pressure-flow relationship:

[ \Delta {=tex}p_{ij} = \operatorname{sign}{=tex}(\dot {=tex}V_{ij}) \left{=tex}|\frac{\dot V_{ij}}{K_{ij}}{=tex}\right{=tex}|^{1/n_{ij}} ]

This relationship makes the nonlinear nature of the airflow network explicit.

1.3. Relationship between mass flow and volumetric flow#

Mass flow rate and volumetric flow rate are related through air density:

[ \dot {=tex}m_{ij}=\rho{=tex}{=tex}V{ij} ]

Here, (\rho{=tex}_{ij}) is the air density (kg/m³), and the volumetric flow rate must be expressed in m³/s to obtain mass flow in kg/s.

Since the flow coefficient in the Python implementation is expressed in m³/h, the conversion is

[ \dot {=tex}m_{ij} = \frac{\rho_{ij}}{3600}{=tex}\dot {=tex}V_{ij} ]

Substituting the power-law constitutive relationship gives

[ \dot {=tex}m_{ij} = \frac{\rho_{ij}K_{ij}}{3600}{=tex} \operatorname{sign}{=tex}(\Delta {=tex}p_{ij}) |\Delta {=tex}p_{ij}|^{n_{ij}} ]

An important consideration is that air density depends on the location from which the air originates.

1.4. Upstream air density#

In an airflow network, the density used to calculate mass flow must correspond to the upstream side of the branch, that is, the node from which the air enters the branch.

For a branch oriented from node (i) to node (j):

  • If (\Delta {=tex}p_{ij}) produces positive flow, air travels from (i) to (j), and the density is (\rho{=tex}_i).

  • If (\Delta {=tex}p_{ij}) produces negative flow, air travels from (j) to (i), and the density is (\rho{=tex}_j).

Therefore, the mass flow relationship becomes

[ \dot {=tex}m_{ij} = \frac{K_{ij}}{3600}{=tex} \rho{=tex}{\mathrm{up}{=tex},ij} \operatorname{sign}{=tex}(\Delta {=tex}p{ij}) |\Delta {=tex}p_{ij}|^{n_{ij}} ]

where (\rho{=tex}_{\mathrm{up}{=tex},ij}) is the density at the upstream end of the branch.

This distinction is particularly important when a branch connects indoor and outdoor environments with different air densities.

In the Python implementation, the upstream density is selected according to the sign of the branch pressure difference. The code stores two possible densities for each branch, corresponding to the two possible flow directions, and selects the appropriate one during each iteration.

2. Pressure sources and flow sources#

An airflow network can be driven by imposed pressure differences and imposed mass flows.

2.1. Pressure sources#

Pressure sources contribute to the pressure difference across a branch independently of the unknown pressure differences between its internal nodes.

Two important sources in building airflow modelling are wind pressure and hydrostatic (static) pressure.

Wind pressure

Wind creates a pressure distribution on the building envelope. The pressure at a particular façade location is commonly expressed as

[ p_{\mathrm{wind}{=tex}}=C_p\frac{\rho_e v^2}{2}{=tex} ]

where:

  • (C_p) is the wind pressure coefficient;

  • (\rho{=tex}_e) is the outdoor air density;

  • (v) is the wind velocity.

The wind pressure coefficient depends on the building geometry, wind direction, and position on the façade. It can be positive or negative, representing positive or suction pressure relative to the reference outdoor pressure.

Static pressure

The hydrostatic pressure variation with height results from the weight of the air column. For a constant density, the pressure variation between a reference height and a height (z) is

[ p_{\mathrm{static}{=tex}}(z)=-\rho {=tex}gz ]

where:

  • (\rho{=tex}) is the air density;

  • (g) is gravitational acceleration;

  • (z) is the vertical coordinate measured relative to the reference level.

When indoor and outdoor air densities differ, the hydrostatic pressure distributions also differ. This produces the stack effect, which can drive infiltration and exfiltration even in the absence of wind.

Combined pressure sources

The pressure sources acting on a branch can combine wind and static pressure contributions. Their algebraic sum is represented by the branch pressure-source vector (\boldsymbol {=tex}b).

The sign of each contribution depends on the orientation assigned to the branch.

2.2. Flow sources#

Flow sources represent imposed mass flows at pressure nodes. Examples include:

  • Mechanical ventilation extraction;

  • Mechanical supply ventilation;

  • Imposed air transfer between zones;

  • Other externally prescribed mass-flow rates.

In the Python code, the mechanical ventilation flow is specified as a volumetric extraction rate and converted into a mass-flow source using the indoor air density.

The resulting mass-flow source is included in the vector (\boldsymbol {=tex}f).

The sign convention must be consistent with the mass balance equation: extraction is represented by a positive sink contribution in the balance equation, while supply is represented by a negative sink contribution.

3. Graph representation of the airflow network#

An airflow network can be represented as an oriented, weighted graph.

  • Pressure nodes are the vertices of the graph.

  • Airflow paths are the edges (branches) connecting the vertices.

  • Each branch has an assigned orientation, which defines the positive direction of airflow.

  • Each branch is associated with a flow conductance that depends on its pressure difference and upstream air density.

The graph representation provides a systematic way to formulate the mass balance equations and the pressure-flow relationships using matrices and vectors.

3.1. Incidence matrix (A)#

The topology of the network is described by the branch-node incidence matrix (A), with dimensions (B\times {=tex}N), where (B) is the number of branches and (N) is the number of internal pressure nodes.

The elements of the matrix are defined as follows:

[ A_{ij} =

]

The orientation of a branch is arbitrary but must remain consistent throughout the formulation.

The reference-pressure node is not included among the unknown pressure nodes. Consequently, a branch connecting an internal node to the reference node has only one nonzero entry in its row of (A).

The incidence matrix is used to express both the branch pressure differences and the mass balance equations.

3.2. Pressure vector and pressure-source vector#

Let (\boldsymbol {=tex}p) denote the vector of unknown pressures at the internal nodes, relative to the reference pressure.

The pressure difference across all branches is calculated using

[ \Delta{=tex}\boldsymbol {=tex}p=-A\boldsymbol {=tex}p+\boldsymbol {=tex}b ]

where:

  • (\boldsymbol {=tex}p) is the vector of internal node pressures (Pa);

  • (\boldsymbol {=tex}b) is the vector of imposed branch pressure sources (Pa);

  • (\Delta{=tex}\boldsymbol {=tex}p) is the vector of branch pressure differences (Pa).

The vector (\boldsymbol {=tex}b) includes the wind and static pressure contributions associated with each branch.

The minus sign in front of (A\boldsymbol {=tex}p) follows the chosen incidence-matrix convention: a branch oriented from node (i) to node (j) has a pressure difference proportional to (p_i-p_j), before adding the imposed pressure source.

3.3. Conductance matrix (G)#

For a linear flow network, the mass flow through a branch is proportional to the pressure difference across it. The proportionality coefficient is its mass-flow conductance.

For a nonlinear airflow network, the equivalent conductance depends on the current pressure difference and the upstream air density.

For branch (i), the equivalent mass-flow conductance is

[ G_i = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{up},i}K_i}{3600}{=tex} |\Delta {=tex}p_i|^{n_i-1} ]

The conductance matrix (G) is diagonal:

[ G=\operatorname{diag}{=tex}(G_1,G_2,\ldots{=tex},G_B) ]

Its diagonal elements have units of kg/(s·Pa).

The mass-flow vector can then be expressed as

[ \dot{\boldsymbol m}{=tex}=G\Delta{=tex}\boldsymbol {=tex}p ]

This expression has the same form as Ohm’s law for an electrical network, where mass flow corresponds to electrical current, pressure difference to voltage, and the inverse of conductance to electrical resistance.

However, unlike a linear electrical resistor, the airflow conductance changes with the pressure difference and can also change when the flow direction reverses and a different upstream density applies.

3.4. Complete matrix formulation#

Using the incidence matrix and conductance matrix, the mass conservation equations become

[ A^TG\Delta{=tex}\boldsymbol {=tex}p+\boldsymbol {=tex}f=0 ]

Substituting the expression for branch pressure differences gives

[ A^TG(-A\boldsymbol {=tex}p+\boldsymbol {=tex}b)+\boldsymbol {=tex}f=0 ]

or, equivalently,

[ A^TGA\boldsymbol {=tex}p=A^TG\boldsymbol {=tex}b+\boldsymbol {=tex}f ]

This equation is the fundamental matrix equation used by the fixed-point algorithm.

It is linear in the unknown pressure vector (\boldsymbol {=tex}p) if (G) is known. However, in the actual airflow network, (G) depends on the pressure differences and upstream densities, which themselves depend on the unknown pressures.

The original problem is therefore nonlinear.

3.5. Role of the vectors and matrices#

The airflow network is completely described by the following quantities:

Symbol Description Dimensions

(A) Branch-node incidence matrix (B\times {=tex}N) (G) Diagonal equivalent conductance matrix (B\times {=tex}B) (\boldsymbol {=tex}b) Branch pressure-source vector (B) (\boldsymbol {=tex}f) Node mass-flow-source vector (N) (\boldsymbol{=tex}\rho{=tex}) Internal-node air-density vector (N) (\rho{=tex}_{\mathrm{ref}{=tex}}) Reference-node air density Scalar (\boldsymbol {=tex}p) Unknown node pressure vector (N)

The density vector and reference-node density are used to determine the upstream density for each branch.

The matrices (A) and (G), together with the vectors (\boldsymbol {=tex}b) and (\boldsymbol {=tex}f), provide the mathematical description of the network topology, branch characteristics, and external conditions.

4. Fixed-point method for solving the nonlinear airflow network#

4.1. Principle of the method#

The difficulty in solving the airflow network comes from the dependence of the conductance matrix (G) on the unknown pressure differences.

The fixed-point method addresses this difficulty by repeatedly solving a linear system in which the conductances are calculated using the pressure estimates from the preceding iteration.

The procedure is:

  1. Assume initial values for the internal node pressures.

  2. Calculate the pressure differences across all branches.

  3. Determine the flow direction and upstream air density for each branch.

  4. Calculate the equivalent conductance of each branch.

  5. Assemble and solve the linear pressure system.

  6. Update the pressure estimates, possibly using under-relaxation.

  7. Calculate the exact nonlinear mass flows and check convergence.

  8. Repeat until the pressure and mass-balance convergence criteria are satisfied.

The method transforms the nonlinear problem into a sequence of linear problems.

4.2. Initialisation#

The algorithm starts with an initial estimate of the internal node pressures:

[ \boldsymbol {=tex}p^{(0)}=\boldsymbol {=tex}p_0 ]

The initial pressures can be estimated from physical considerations, previous calculations, or simply assigned reasonable values.

The branch orientations are defined by the incidence matrix (A).

For each branch, the code identifies the two possible upstream densities:

  • Density at the starting node for positive flow;

  • Density at the ending node for negative flow.

If one end of the branch is the reference-pressure node, the density at that end is taken from (\rho{=tex}_{\mathrm{ref}{=tex}}).

These two densities are stored before starting the iterations.

4.3. Calculation of pressure differences#

At iteration (k), the branch pressure differences are calculated from the current pressure estimates:

[ \Delta{=tex}\boldsymbol {=tex}p^{(k)} = -A\boldsymbol {=tex}p^{(k)}+\boldsymbol {=tex}b ]

The sign of each component determines the direction of airflow.

A positive pressure difference corresponds to flow in the assigned branch orientation, whereas a negative pressure difference corresponds to flow in the opposite direction.

4.4. Selection of upstream density#

For each branch, the upstream density is selected according to the sign of its pressure difference:

[ \rho{=tex}_{\mathrm{up}{=tex},i}^{(k)} =

]

This operation is important because the density used to calculate mass flow changes when the airflow reverses.

The density selection is implemented in Python using:

rho_up = np.where(d >= 0, rho_forward, rho_backward)

4.5. Calculation of equivalent conductances#

Using the pressure differences and upstream densities obtained at iteration (k), the equivalent conductance of each branch is calculated as

[ G_i^{(k)} = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{up},i}^{(k)}K_i}{3600}{=tex} \left{=tex}|\Delta {=tex}p_i^{(k)}\right{=tex}|^{n_i-1} ]

For numerical stability, the implementation introduces a minimum pressure difference (d_{\mathrm{floor}{=tex}}):

[ G_i^{(k)} = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{up},i}^{(k)}K_i}{3600}{=tex} \left[ \max\left( |\Delta p_i^{(k)}|,d_{\mathrm{floor}} \right) \right]{=tex}^{n_i-1} ]

This avoids excessively large conductances when the pressure difference approaches zero, particularly for flow exponents smaller than one.

The resulting diagonal conductance matrix is

[ G^{(k)} = \operatorname{diag}{=tex} \left{=tex}( G_1^{(k)},\ldots{=tex},G_B^{(k)} \right{=tex}) ]

The conductance matrix is therefore updated at every iteration.

4.6. Solution of the linear pressure system#

Once the conductance matrix is known, the nonlinear problem is approximated by a linear problem.

The mass balance equations are written as

[ A^TG^{(k)}A\boldsymbol {=tex}p_{\mathrm{linear}{=tex}}^{(k+1)} = A^TG^{(k)}\boldsymbol {=tex}b+\boldsymbol {=tex}f ]

The new pressure estimate is obtained by solving this system:

[ \boldsymbol {=tex}p_{\mathrm{linear}{=tex}}^{(k+1)} = \left{=tex}(A^TG^{(k)}A\right{=tex})^{-1} \left{=tex}(A^TG^{(k)}\boldsymbol {=tex}b+\boldsymbol {=tex}f\right{=tex}) ]

In the Python implementation, the inverse is not explicitly calculated. Instead, the linear system is solved using:

M = A.T @ G @ A
rhs = A.T @ G @ b + f

p_linear = np.linalg.solve(M, rhs)

This is preferable numerically to explicitly calculating the inverse matrix.

4.7. Under-relaxation#

Directly replacing the previous pressure estimate with the solution of the linear system can sometimes produce oscillations or convergence difficulties.

To improve numerical stability, the algorithm uses under-relaxation:

[ \boldsymbol {=tex}p^{(k+1)} = (1-\omega{=tex})\boldsymbol {=tex}p^{(k)} + \omega{=tex}\boldsymbol {=tex}p_{\mathrm{linear}{=tex}}^{(k+1)} ]

where (\omega{=tex}) is the relaxation coefficient:

  • (0<\omega{=tex}<1): under-relaxation;

  • (\omega=1{=tex}): no relaxation;

  • Smaller values of (\omega{=tex}) produce more conservative pressure updates.

The code uses (\omega=0.5{=tex}) by default.

Under-relaxation can improve convergence when successive pressure estimates oscillate, although excessively small values can make convergence unnecessarily slow.

4.8. Calculation of exact nonlinear mass flows#

After updating the pressures, the algorithm calculates the mass flow rates using the original nonlinear constitutive relationship, rather than the equivalent linear conductances.

For each branch:

[ \dot {=tex}m_i^{(k+1)} = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{up},i}^{(k+1)}K_i}{3600}{=tex} \operatorname{sign}{=tex}(\Delta {=tex}p_i^{(k+1)}) |\Delta {=tex}p_i^{(k+1)}|^{n_i} ]

The use of the exact nonlinear relationship is essential for checking whether the solution satisfies the original airflow network equations.

The mass-balance residual is then calculated:

[ \boldsymbol {=tex}r_m^{(k+1)} = A^T\dot{\boldsymbol m}{=tex}^{(k+1)} +\boldsymbol {=tex}f ]

The maximum absolute residual is

[ \varepsilon{=tex}m^{(k+1)} = |\boldsymbol {=tex}r_m^{(k+1)}|\infty{=tex} ]

This measures the largest mass imbalance among all internal pressure nodes.

4.9. Convergence criteria#

The algorithm uses two independent convergence criteria.

Pressure convergence

The relative change in pressure between two successive iterations is calculated as

[ \varepsilon{=tex}_p^{(k+1)} = \frac{ \|\boldsymbol p^{(k+1)}-\boldsymbol p^{(k)}\|_\infty }{ \max\left( p_{\mathrm{scale}}, \|\boldsymbol p^{(k+1)}\|_\infty \right) }{=tex} ]

The parameter (p_{\mathrm{scale}{=tex}}) prevents the relative pressure error from becoming excessively restrictive when all node pressures are close to zero.

Mass conservation convergence

The mass-balance residual must satisfy

[ \varepsilon{=tex}_m^{(k+1)}<\mathrm{tol}{=tex}_m ]

The pressure change must also satisfy

[ \varepsilon{=tex}_p^{(k+1)}<\mathrm{tol}{=tex}_p ]

The algorithm terminates only when both conditions are satisfied:

[ \varepsilon{=tex}_p<\mathrm{tol}{=tex}_p \quad{=tex}\text{and}{=tex}\quad{=tex} \varepsilon{=tex}_m<\mathrm{tol}{=tex}_m ]

This dual criterion is important. A small pressure change does not necessarily mean that the nonlinear mass-balance equations are satisfied. Checking both criteria ensures that the solution is sufficiently stable and conserves mass.

If the convergence criteria are not satisfied within the maximum number of iterations, the algorithm raises an error and reports the final pressure error and mass-balance residual.

5. Summary of the algorithm#

The complete fixed-point procedure can be summarised as follows.

INPUT:
    A, b, f, K, n, rho, rho_ref
    Initial pressures p0
    Relaxation coefficient omega
    Convergence tolerances tol_p, tol_m

INITIALISATION:
    p = p0
    Determine forward and backward densities
    for each branch

ITERATION:

    1. Calculate branch pressure differences
       d = -A @ p + b

    2. Determine upstream density
       rho_up = upstream_density(d)

    3. Calculate equivalent conductances
       g = rho_up * K / 3600
           * max(abs(d), d_floor)**(n - 1)

    4. Assemble the linear system
       M = A.T @ G @ A
       rhs = A.T @ G @ b + f

    5. Solve for new pressures
       p_linear = solve(M, rhs)

    6. Apply under-relaxation
       p_new = (1 - omega)*p + omega*p_linear

    7. Check pressure convergence
       err_p = relative_pressure_change(p_new, p)

    8. Calculate exact nonlinear mass flows
       m_dot = mass_flow(p_new)

    9. Check mass conservation
       residual = A.T @ m_dot + f
       err_m = max(abs(residual))

   10. Update pressures
       p = p_new

   11. Stop if both convergence criteria
       are satisfied.

OUTPUT:
    Node pressures p
    Branch mass flows m_dot
    Branch volumetric flows V_dot

Once convergence is achieved, the volumetric flow rates are calculated using the original power-law relationship:

[ \dot {=tex}V_i = K_i\operatorname{sign}{=tex}(\Delta {=tex}p_i) |\Delta {=tex}p_i|^{n_i} ]

The function returns three quantities:

  • (\boldsymbol {=tex}p): internal node pressures (Pa);

  • (\dot{\boldsymbol m}{=tex}): branch mass flow rates (kg/s);

  • (\dot{\boldsymbol V}{=tex}): branch volumetric flow rates (m³/h).

6. Interpretation and characteristics of the method#

The fixed-point method can be interpreted as a successive linearisation of the nonlinear airflow network.

At each iteration, the current pressure differences determine the equivalent conductances. These conductances define a linear network, which is solved to obtain updated node pressures. The procedure is repeated until the linearised solution is consistent with the original nonlinear constitutive laws and the mass conservation equations.

Its main characteristics are:

  1. Physical formulation: Mass conservation is enforced at every internal pressure node, while the constitutive laws describe the nonlinear behaviour of individual airflow paths.

  2. Graph-based formulation: The incidence matrix describes the network topology, while the diagonal conductance matrix describes the current hydraulic (or, more precisely, pneumatic) properties of its branches.

  3. Variable density: The upstream density is updated according to the direction of airflow, allowing the method to account for different indoor and outdoor air densities.

  4. Linear systems: Each iteration requires solving a linear system, rather than solving the entire nonlinear system simultaneously.

  5. Under-relaxation: The relaxation coefficient provides a means of controlling the pressure updates and improving numerical stability.

  6. Physical convergence check: The algorithm verifies both pressure convergence and mass conservation using the exact nonlinear mass-flow equations.

The method is particularly useful for building airflow networks involving infiltration, exfiltration, stack effect, wind pressure, and mechanical ventilation.

An important numerical consideration: The method is a fixed-point iteration, not a Newton method. Its convergence is not guaranteed for every network or every initial pressure estimate. The choice of relaxation coefficient, initial pressures, and treatment of very small pressure differences can influence convergence. Furthermore, the pressure matrix must be nonsingular, which requires the internal nodes to be adequately connected to a reference-pressure node through the network.
